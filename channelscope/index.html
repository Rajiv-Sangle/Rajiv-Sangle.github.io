In skeletal muscle cells, RyR1 releases the calcium that sets off contraction, letting it out of the cell's internal store. It belongs to the ryanodine receptors, the largest ion channels known.An ion channel is a protein, or a complex of proteins, that spans a membrane and forms a water-filled channel through which ions, charged atoms, cross it. Human RyR1 is four copies of one chain of 5,038 amino acids, 20,152 in all, together more than 2.2 million daltons.

RyR1's name traces back to a plant. In the 18th century the Danish botanist Martin Vahl named a South American plant Ryania speciosa, after John Ryan, who had sent him the sample. The insecticide in it was isolated in 1948 and named ryanodine, and radiolabelled ryanodine later allowed the protein it binds, the "ryanodine receptor", to be purified and identified.

Seen from the cytoplasm in , the four copies, called subunits, sit around a central pore. Seen from the side, most of the channel is a cap in the cytosol, the part of the cell's contents outside its nucleus and other organelles, and a stalk crosses the membrane of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the cell's calcium store. The channel works by changing shape between several states, with its pore closed in some and open in others, and what binds it does not by itself decide its state.

This article explains what RyR1 is and how it works, how its gene maps to the protein, what kinds of change there are and how some are linked to disease, following one change from Section 2 on, and ends with what ChannelScope does today and what it does not yet do. Through it runs the question ChannelScope starts from, what a missense variant, one that changes a single amino acid, does to RyR1's structure and function; ChannelScope is a first step toward evidence that keeps its provenance, takes in new sources without rewriting, can be composed around a residue and carried over to other proteins, and is of use in choosing variants to test and as data for machine-learning models.

How RyR1 works

showed what RyR1 looks like. This section follows what it does in a muscle cell, how its parts are arranged, and how it moves between states.

What it does. RyR1 releases calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the muscle cell's internal store, into the cytosol, and that release triggers contraction. When a muscle cell is stimulated, the rapid release of calcium from this store is an essential step of excitation–contraction coupling.

Ryanodine, the compound the ryanodine receptors are named for, binds the channel: at low concentrations it locks the channel in a state that conducts less than a fully open one; at higher concentrations it blocks it.
What opens it, and what tunes it

The signal to open. In skeletal muscle, the signal to open comes from the cell's surface membrane. A voltage-sensing calcium channel there, CaV1.1 (the dihydropyridine receptor), is thought to be coupled to RyR1 physically, so that RyR1 opens without calcium having to rise first. Not every RyR1 is coupled. In the membrane, every other channel faces a group of four CaV1.1 channels; the rest are thought to be opened by calcium released by their neighbours, or perhaps through their contacts with neighbouring channels. In the heart, RyR1's relative RyR2Mammals have three ryanodine receptors. RyR1 is found mostly in skeletal muscle, RyR2 mostly in the heart, and RyR3 widely at low levels, and each also occurs in other tissues: UniProt lists RyR1 in skeletal muscle and the brain. The three are roughly two-thirds identical in sequence; published estimates run from 63% to 70%. is opened by calcium itself, which is called calcium-induced calcium release; only RyR1 is opened by coupling to the surface membrane's voltage sensor.

Tuning. The channel's opening is tuned by calcium, ATP and magnesium, by compounds such as caffeine, and by proteins: among them calmodulin, and FKBP12, which is thought to steady the closed channel. Calcium both opens and closes the channel. In recordings of channels set in artificial membranes, opening rises with calcium to a peak at roughly 10 to 100 micromolar,A millimolar, micromolar or nanomolar concentration is a thousandth, a millionth or a billionth of a mole per litre. then falls at higher levels: a negative feedback. The concentrations depend on the isoform, and on the other small molecules and proteins present.

    The cap. Most of the cytoplasmic part is a long scaffold of repeating helical units, called α-solenoids, with compact domains set into it; by one review's count, the solenoids make up about half of the whole protein.

    Along the chain. lays the chain itself out, from residue 1 to residue 5,038, with a selection of what UniProt's entry for human RyR1 records along it. Its first track is the topology. In UniProt's terms, a membrane protein's topology says which stretches of its chain cross the membrane, which lie within it without crossing it, and in which compartment each of the others lies: for RyR1, the cytoplasm or the lumen of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

    Topology. Point at a feature, or tap it, to read UniProt's record: in its topology the chain is cytoplasmic up to residue 4,559, and its six helices across the membrane, with the pore-forming stretch between the last two, lie between residues 4,560 and 4,941.

    Repeats. UniProt marks six stretches of the chain as copies of one repeated sequence, "approximate" because they "deviate significantly from the consensus sequence", and the stretch from 841 to 2,959 as the region that holds them. Repeats 3 and 4, which it calls "truncated", are two short, partial copies of 16 residues each.

    Regions. Each region is named for a protein that RyR1 interacts with there. FKBP1A is the gene of FKBP12, also called calstabin 1, a small protein bound to RyR1; UniProt marks residues 669 to 680 by similarity to rabbit RyR1. CALM is calmodulin, a small protein that binds calcium and that three human genes, CALM1, CALM2 and CALM3, make with the same sequence; UniProt marks residues 3,614 to 3,643 from an experiment.

    S0, or TMx. Along the chain, an extra membrane helix, residues 4,324 to 4,341, comes first, about 220 residues before S1 and to the left of the enlarged stretch; UniProt's topology counts that part of the chain as cytoplasmic and does not show it. Des Georges and colleagues call it TMx (TM for transmembrane), and Wei and colleagues S0, and it is seen in some structures and not in others.

    In the membrane. Then come S1 to S4, what des Georges and colleagues call a pseudo-voltage-sensor domain; then S5; then the pore-forming stretch, a pore helix and the selectivity filter, which dips into the membrane from the lumen without crossing it; then S6. With those of the other three subunits, S5, the pore-forming stretch and S6 form the pore, which the S6 helices and the selectivity filter line. Zalk and colleagues place this fold in the superfamily of six-transmembrane ion channels that includes the voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels.

    The gate. In a closed structure, the four S6 helices close the pore where it meets the cytosol.

    How it moves

    Activation and gating. As des Georges and colleagues define them, activation is an activator raising the chance that the channel is open, and gating is the channel's movement between closed and open. A 2025 study sorts its structures into four states, and draws them.

    Four states. Choose a state in the figure. Resting is closed, with no calcium; primed is still closed, but the cytoplasmic part has changed shape; activated is open; and inhibited is closed again. In that 2025 study, the state depended on the calcium and on S0, the extra helix in the membrane that labels TMx: a gentler purification kept it in the sample, and the usual one lost it. With S0 lost, nanomolar calcium, or micromolar calcium with ATP, left the channel primed. With S0 kept, nanomolar calcium left part of the sample primed and opened the rest, micromolar calcium with ATP opened it, and millimolar calcium left it inhibited.Other studies reached the primed state under other conditions: a 2016 study with 30 micromolar calcium alone, or with ATP and caffeine and no calcium. Papers name the last state differently, and Nayak and Samsó call it closed-inactivated. The four names simplify: the channel takes many shapes.

    How it opens. Opening involves a movement of the whole cytoplasmic part, accompanied by changes in the membrane, where the pore's inner helices bend outward and the pore widens. Calcium, ATP and caffeine bind where the C-terminal domain meets its neighbours; that small domain hangs into the cytosol from the end of the pore's inner helix.

    How a structure is made. A structure is made by single-particle cryo-electron microscopy from many thousands of molecules held in one condition, picked one by one from the images and sorted into classes; one structure stands for one class. A condition sets only the chance of opening, so one sample can yield both open and closed structures.In recordings under one such condition, calcium with ATP and caffeine, the channel was open 91% of the time; in the structures from that condition, about half the particles, the single molecules imaged, were open. The two numbers are read two ways: des Georges and colleagues read the half as compatible with an open probability that is high but short of one, while a 2022 review notes that the detergent in the sample can significantly alter how often the channel opens, so the two need not match.

    One question is still open

    With only the body's own activators, calcium and ATP, most structures reach the primed state and stop, and most open structures were obtained with a drug such as caffeine added. Two groups report exceptions: an open structure with calcium and an ATP analogue, in a lipid environment, in 2022, and in 2025 open structures from samples that kept S0. One of them describes the gap between structures and recordings as "a mystery"; a 2022 review holds it most likely a result of how the samples are prepared, with detergents a major culprit.

    From gene to residue

    Spelled out in the gene. shows RyR1, here the rabbit's, as four chains of amino acids that together form one channel. Each of the 5,038 amino acids of human RyR1, in the form NM_000540.3 encodes, is spelled out in the gene's coding sequence, three bases at a time, so a change in those bases can change an amino acid.

    Naming a change. A change in an exon can be written against one of the gene's transcripts: c.1840C>T says that the 1,840th base of the coding sequence of NM_000540.3, a C, became a T. A transcript is an RNA made on a DNA template, here the gene's, by an RNA polymerase, an enzyme.

    Tracing a change. Before one can ask what such a change might do to the channel, it has to be traced from the gene to the protein: in which exon it lies, in which codon, and which amino acid, if any, it alters. A codon is a set of three bases in the coding sequence that together code for one amino acid, or for the end of the protein's chain.

    What kinds of change there are

    A variant is a difference from a reference sequence; c.1840C>T, from the last section, is one. It can be sorted in two ways: by what happens to the DNA's letters, and by what that does to the protein's sequence.

    Substitution, one amino acid changed. opens on c.1840C>T: one letter replaced by one other, a substitution, that changes one amino acid. Read in threes under the table, codon 614, CGC, becomes TGC, and arginine becomes cysteine. Its p. name, p.(Arg614Cys), like every p. name here, is computed by from the rules and sequences in release exon_map_v0.5.3; it is a prediction, which HGVS writes "in parentheses".

    One letter, three outcomes. In the coding sequence, one changed letter can change its codon's amino acid: a missense change, as c.1840C>T is. It can leave the amino acid unchanged, a synonymous change, since several codons read one amino acid: HGVS's table lists arginine as "AGA, AGG, CGA, CGC, CGG, CGT". So changing the third letter instead, as the computed c.1842C>T does, gives CGT, still arginine. Or it can turn the codon into a stop, a stop gained, as c.10171G>T does; ClinVarClinVar is a public archive, kept at the US National Institutes of Health, of human genetic variants and of interpretations of their significance to disease. records its molecular consequence as nonsense, HGVS's word for the kind.ClinVar's record VCV002710027.6, as retrieved on 29 September 2026. Point at c.1840C>T and c.1842C>T in turn: one codon, a different letter changed, a different kind.

    Deletions and duplications. Letters can also be lost or added. If their number is a multiple of three, the reading frame is kept, and whole amino acids are usually removed or added: c.51_53del loses an aspartic acid, and c.992_994dup adds a glutamic acid. Any other number shifts the frame, so the codons after it are read wrongly, usually until a stop. c.13013_13032del, which loses twenty letters, is a frameshift. Choose the three in turn: the codons after the change stay in step, then fall out of step.Behind "usually": an in-frame change can still make a stop, as the computed c.1843_1844insAAT would; and HGVS says "deletions starting 5' of and including the translation termination (stop) codon are described as frameshift".

    Other kinds, the same outcome. The two sortings are independent. An inversion replaces more than one letter with their reverse complement: "an inversion would change AAGC to GCTT", says HGVS, whereas "CGAA is only the reverse of AAGC". The ClinVar records c.14422_14423inv, an inversion, and c.13994_13995delinsCT, a deletion-insertion, each change one amino acid, as the substitution c.1840C>T does.     Start or stop changed. At the protein's ends, a change to the start codon, a start lost, is written p.(Met1?). That is HGVS's form when the consequence "can not be predicted (i.e. is unknown)". c.1A>G is one, and it has been seen among the people whose sequences gnomADgnomAD is "a resource developed by an international coalition of investigators, with the goal of aggregating and harmonizing both exome and genome sequencing data from a wide variety of large-scale sequencing projects, and making summary data available for the wider scientific community". gathers.As retrieved on 6 October 2026, with at least one copy passing filters.     Outside the coding sequence. Where a change falls decides much. One in an intron is an intron variant, and one in an exon's untranslated end a 5′ or 3′ UTR variant; the ClinVar record c.-108T>C lies in the 5′ untranslated end. Only a change in the coding sequence alters the protein's sequence directly. "Directly" is the limit. The release says: "Splicing, the transcript's fate, and any effect on the protein's structure or function are not computed."

    At an exon's edge. Splicing removes the introns: an exon is a region "not removed from the primary RNA transcript by RNA splicing". A change at an exon's edge may sit in a splice site, the intron's "2 bp region bordering the exon" at either end, or in the wider splice region, "within 1-3 bases of the exon or 3-8 bases of the intron"; the release's rules flag it, and the flag "predicts nothing". The ClinVar record c.14364+1G>T lies in an intron, so it is named on the genome, NC_000019.10(NM_000540.3):c.14364+1G>T, and the release's rules flag it at the donor site.     What a kind does not say. Every kind above is read from the sequence: the release labels each one "computed from the sequence; not a clinical interpretation". A kind says what a change does to the letters, the codons and the protein's sequence. It does not say whether the change causes disease, nor what it does to the protein's structure or function. Of these kinds, ChannelScope's version 0.1 reads one, the missense change. That follows from the question it starts from, what a change of a single amino acid does to the channel's structure and function, and from the rule that chose its 299 residues: at least one of ClinVar's expert-panel records describes a single missense change there.     How some changes are linked to disease

    Evidence, change by change. Whether a particular change goes with a disease is a separate question from its kind, answered change by change from evidence about it: in families, in the channel's behaviour in cells or tissue, and in how often it turns up in patients. Expert groups weigh that evidence by written rules.

    Four conditions, two wordings. UniProt's entry for human RyR1 (entry version 256, as retrieved on 29 September 2026) lists four conditions, and its words differ by condition. For malignant hyperthermia it writes that "Disease susceptibility is associated with variants"; for the other three, that "The disease is caused by variants".

    Two ingredients. Malignant hyperthermia, MH, is "a disorder that requires a combination of a genetic mutation and a pharmacological agent", typically a volatile anaesthetic or a muscle relaxant such as succinylcholine, and "In some cases, stress may serve as an alternative external trigger". Switch on a genetic change, then a trigger: an episode becomes possible only with both. With the change alone, the person is susceptible: MH susceptibility, or MHS, which the sources keep apart from the condition.

    Symptoms and treatment. In an episode, patients "experience a hypermetabolic state, acidosis, and a dangerous increase in body temperature": the body's chemistry races, acid builds up, the body overheats. "If untreated, MH has a high mortality rate, but administration of dantrolene can reduce this to <5%".

    What goes wrong in the channel? Studies of many MH variants "generally agree on the overall gain-of-function phenotype, with an increased sensitivity of mutant RyR1 to triggering agents such as Ca2+, caffeine, and volatile anesthetics", a 2022 review writes; in plain words, the changed channel is more sensitive to the agents that trigger it. In an episode, an older review explains, "an excessive leak of Ca2+ from the SR results in a hypermetabolic state"; the SR, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, is the muscle cell's calcium store (Section 1). That is the reviews' summary, not shown for every variant.

    Muscle diseases. Two of the other conditions are what UniProt names congenital myopathies, 1A and 1B, muscle diseases that UniProt describes as "beginning in infancy or early childhood" (dominant) or "apparent soon after birth or in early childhood" (recessive). Central core disease (CCD) "is the main myopathy associated with RyR1", a 2022 review writes, with "muscle weakness, often progressive", and "Very often, CCD patients are also prone to MH." Unlike MH, an older review notes, "CCD does not require an external trigger".

    Why "central core". The disease takes its name from "dense cores of metabolically inactive tissue, devoid of mitochondria, often in the center of skeletal muscle tissue".

    Inheritance. "Depending on the exact mutation, CCD can be inherited in an autosomal dominant or recessive fashion",Dominant. A person with one altered copy of the gene in each cell can be affected by an autosomal dominant disorder. Recessive. In autosomal recessive inheritance, variants occur in both copies of the gene in each cell, and the parents of a person with such a condition each carry one altered copy but typically show no signs or symptoms of it. and MH susceptibility "predominantly shows autosomal dominant inheritance, although not all reported cases are consistent with simple autosomal dominant inheritance".

    Two kinds of subunit. RyR1 is four copies of one protein, its subunits. Someone who carries a change in one of their two copies of the RYR1 gene can make two kinds of subunit, and in cells made to produce both, one channel can hold both kinds.

    Four copies, possibly of two kinds

    In such cells, single channels can contain both kinds: their electrical behaviour falls between that of the two pure forms. Every such experiment was done in cultured cells making the protein in large amounts, and counting a channel as mixed assumes that the subunits combine at random.

    None of these studies measured whether mixed channels form in a person's muscle, or how many. A study of how a potassium channel's subunits assemble warns that producing a protein in large amounts may itself make its subunits mix. The often-quoted figure of 1 in 16 fully normal channels is arithmetic on two assumptions: that both copies of the gene are made equally, and that the subunits combine at random. The two copies are not always made equally either. Measured in patients' muscle and in cells grown from patients, the ratio runs from about half and half to one copy silent, and every such measurement is of the gene's message, its RNA; none is of the protein.

    A changed subunit does not simply spoil a channel either. Its effect depends on how many changed subunits a channel holds, and differs from one change to another; in a study of one such change, mice carrying one changed copy of the gene showed no significant deficit.

    Every structure in this article has four identical subunits. Those cited here were purified from the skeletal muscle of rabbits, mice or pigs. Even the structures of channels that carry a disease-linked change have four changed subunits: they come from animals that carry the change on both copies of the gene, or from cultured cells that make only the changed subunit.

    Back to c.1840C>T. The change this article follows, c.1840C>T, changes arginine 614 to cysteine, and its story began in pigs. A 2022 review recounts that "a single point mutation in pig RyR1, R615C, is responsible for PSS", porcine stress syndrome, and that "Soon thereafter, the corresponding mutation in human RyR1, R614C, was linked to MH".

    In pig channels. Woll and colleagues later solved structures of RyR1 from pigs that carry R615C on both copies of the gene, "under nominally Ca2+-free conditions (5 mM EGTA)", in which their own recordings in lipid bilayers "indicate the channel should be closed". In the cryo-EM sample, "For wild-type pRyR1, the major class still represents closed channels, but for R615C pRyR1 the combination of cryo-EM condition and mutation results in mostly open channels" (pRyR1: pig RyR1).Wild-type. In Woll et al.'s pig study, RyR1 from pig muscle in which the R615C change was absent. These are pig channels under conditions the authors count as activating, not a human channel in a person's muscle.

    A genetic result's limits. EMHG writes that "Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants can be used for the diagnosis of MH susceptibility", but that "absence of RYR1 and CACNA1S variants does not exclude MH. The only possibility to establish an MH negative diagnosis is in-vitro contracture testing."CACNA1S. The gene that encodes CaV1.1, the muscle's voltage-gated calcium channel. A genetic result can support a diagnosis, then, but cannot rule one out. Johnston and colleagues wrote in 2022 that "About three-fourths of MH susceptible families have pathogenic variants in RYR1".

    How many people carry such a variant? Among gnomAD's people, Johnston and colleagues "estimated the prevalence of individuals with RYR1-related MHS pathogenic variants to be between 1/300 and 1/1075, considerably higher than current estimates". Some people with a particular variant do not develop features of the disorder.

    What ChannelScope does today

    Take a change at one residue, such as c.1840C>T, which changes arginine 614 to cysteine. What can be said, from evidence, about what it does to the channel, and what cannot?

    To see where a change sits, one needs the channel's structure, and the channel in is not a human one. As of 6 October 2026 the Protein Data BankThe Protein Data Bank is the single worldwide archive of experimentally determined three-dimensional structures of proteins, DNA and RNA, free to use. Its two entries with human RyR1 sequence, 6UHI and 6UHS, are each a crystal structure of a chimera that fuses 114 amino acids of human RyR1 to a protein of the bacterium Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron. held no experimental structure of the whole human channel, and as of 5 October 2026 AlphaFold's database held no model of it, and public folding tools, by their documented limits, could not build one.AlphaFold's database, asked through its API, returned no model for human, rabbit or pig RyR1, and the documented limits of public folding tools fall below one four-chain channel of 20,152 amino acids: AlphaFold Server counts one token per amino acid and takes up to 5,000 a job, fewer than one chain's 5,038; AlphaFold 3's own documentation gives up to 5,120 tokens on one 80-gigabyte GPU; and CombFold was tested on assemblies of up to 30 chains and 18,000 amino acids. What has been determined, in several states, is the structure of the channel in other mammals, among them rabbit, mouse and pig, including pigs that carry R615C, the counterpart of human R614C.

    So, in ChannelScope's reading, the hard part is bookkeeping and judgement: matching residue numbers between species, knowing which state each structure shows, and knowing what a structure can and cannot say. A structure can show where a change sits, where its model is reliable (version 0.1 does not assess that), and structures in different states can show how its surroundings move between them. A structure of the normal channel cannot show in which direction a change pushes the channel (towards gain or loss of function); that takes laboratory tests, which exist for few variants: of the 251 that ClinGen's panel classified in 2022, functional data meeting its criteria were found for 13. Whether a change is harmful is weighed from families, patients and tests together (Section 4).

    ChannelScope brings the evidence together around a residue. It says where each value came from, and where sources disagree, it keeps their values side by side instead of merging them into one verdict. Its aim, an aspiration rather than a promise, is evidence that keeps its provenance, takes in new sources without rewriting, can be composed around a residue and carried over to other proteins, and is of use in choosing variants to test and as data for machine-learning models; the table at the end of this section gives, for each part, what version 0.1 shows and what is still a premise, with the test it awaits. lays out version 0.1 in eight steps, from the question it starts from to one count.

    Which residue is it inside each structure?

    In ChannelScope's README: 2. The residue numbering of 26 structures

    Inside a structure, residues carry the numbers their depositors, the scientists who determined it, gave them, so ChannelScope checks each structure's numbering against UniProt, in two ways where it can: with SIFTS, the European Bioinformatics Institute's map from each residue of a Protein Data Bank entry to UniProt, and with its own alignment.     Open the pig structures in the figure: at pig 615, the partner of human 614, four of the seven carry cysteine, as in R615C.     Is each structure open or closed at the gate?

    In ChannelScope's README: 3. The pore at the gate of the same 26 structures

    Each structure comes with its state at the gate, since what a variant does can depend on the channel's state. Here a structure's state means the class of its gate's ring, closed or open, not the conformation of the whole channel. Section 4 gave the reason the gate matters for disease: the changes linked to malignant hyperthermia generally make the channel more sensitive to what opens it, and for pig channels with R615C, in Woll and colleagues' words, "the combination of cryo-EM condition and mutation results in mostly open channels", where for normal ones "the major class still represents closed channels". One sample can yield both open and closed structures, and papers name states differently, as Section 1 showed, so ChannelScope measures the clearance of the gate's ring, by two implementations, and quotes beside it the words that name the structure's state, where a source names one.The words come from the entry's title, the depositors' paper, a companion entry's record for 7M6A, or a review for the two positive controls, 5TB0 and 5T9V. For three, 8VJJ, 8VJK and 6W1N, the titles give none, and the release records no words naming their state; the paper of 8VJJ and 8VJK, whose passages on the pore it did not record, calls both pores closed. Each structure's words and measured class are set side by side, with no count of matches: which words imply a state was not fixed before the measurement. The clearance is the least distance from the pore's axis to any heavy atom of the four gate isoleucines, less that atom's radius.The axis is taken through the centre of the four isoleucines' Cα atoms, perpendicular to the plane that best fits them, and the radius is the atom's van der Waals radius (carbon 1.70 Å in the release's code). It is not the pore's narrowest radius, which in an open structure may lie elsewhere along the pore: for 9ZOE, which its authors call open, they report about 3.0 Å by HOLE, a program that measures a pore's radius along its length, where this measure gives 5.28 Å. By the first implementation, the 26 clearances fall in two groups with nothing between them, closed from 0.83 to 1.52 Å (14 structures) and open from 3.20 to 5.55 Å (12), either side of class lines at 2.0 and 3.0 Å, ChannelScope's own, committed before the first measurement, as the release's ledger attests; a clearance between the lines would be indeterminate.The two positive controls sit well inside, 5TB0 at 1.01 Å and 5T9V at 4.16 Å. Sixteen of the 26 were chosen as closed and open pairs (six named before any measurement, and 9ZO8 and 9ZOE) or as positive controls, so a gap between the groups is partly built in by the choice. Eight of the 26 are structures of changed channels: four carry Y523S and four R615C, which 9PWO declares and 6X34 to 6X36 do not; their classes count with the others'. Here "closed" and "open" mean narrow or wide at the gate's ring of isoleucines, not whether ions flow.

    For two structures, the class depends on where the lines are drawn. 7M6A, the structure of , becomes indeterminate if they move by −0.5 Å; its paper calls it a "primed model", and the record of its companion entry, 7M6L, the open model from the same dataset, calls 7M6A a "Closed state model". 6X34 becomes indeterminate if they move by +0.5 Å; it is "open" in its title and "intermediate" in its paper.Read by the second implementation as it was first run, before the re-measurement told below, 9NMP and 6X35 would also become indeterminate if the lines moved by +0.5 Å. The local quality of each structure's atomic model is not assessed, and every structure row says so.

    Where each value came from

    In ChannelScope's README: How ChannelScope works

    Choose any value, and panel B follows it back, step by step, to its sources. draws that path for one value, the 9PWO structure's line at pig 615, through the records it passes. ChannelScope assembles its evidence in steps, and each step leaves a record. It downloads a file from a public source and logs the download: its address, its time, the source's release where it states one, and the file's checksum, a fingerprint that changes if the file does. A run of its code then follows a written method to make a table from such files, and the table's record names every input, by its identifier or its checksum, and the run and the method it followed, whose own record gives its code's version. A last run composes the tables around each residue, and each row it writes names the records it came from.

    These records are the nodes of ChannelScope's evidence graph, and its links are named in the terms of PROV-O, a standard vocabulary for provenance from the World Wide Web Consortium: a table is made by a run (wasGeneratedBy); the run follows a method (PROV-O's Plan) and uses its inputs (used); each record is credited to an agent (wasAttributedTo), and a correction names the record it revises (wasRevisionOf). A record's identifier is computed from what it says, and the validator, a program that checks every record against the rules of ChannelScope's schema, flags a record that no longer matches its identifier. The three releases behind keep their evidence this way. The two that the figures of Sections 1 to 3 read, UniProt's features of RyR1 and the exon map, keep none: each one's manifest gives its files' checksums, and the exon map's exon rows were copied from records of ChannelScope's evidence graph that are not published.

    Kept this way, evidence from separate sources sits side by side around one residue, each value still traceable to where it came from, and a correction keeps the record it replaces. So a reader can check a value instead of trusting it, as far as each step can be repeated: follow it back as does; fetch each file again and compare its checksum, which a re-run does itself, refusing a file that differs; for the results that can be re-run, run the method again on the same inputs, which writes records with the same identifiers; and let the validator flag any record changed since. On the drawn path, the gate's re-measurement and the composition can be re-run from the repository; the mapping's build and the numbering check cannot yet, since the mapping's input lists and the numbering check's code are not published. Two methods on the path, the numbering check's and the gate re-measurement's, are withheld, since they cite the project's private working records; and the trace stops at the public source: ChannelScope records what came back, and when, and does not vouch for the source.

    In ChannelScope's README: Checking it yourself and Re-running the results

    Each check's criterion, which says what would count as a failure, was committed before the run it judges, and every outcome is reported as it came out, failures included. On 15 October 2026 a re-run of every command, on Windows and on Linux, found every file's checksum as listed and every result the same, apart from three readings of the gate, which the release allows to differ from one computer to another. The three readings: the sign of a height along an axis whose direction is arbitrary, for 8X4A and 8X4C, and the first run's window for 6X33. On Windows the gate's first attempt stopped when a download from the Protein Data Bank broke off mid-file, and passed when run again. The repository's README names what cannot yet be re-run from it, such as the numbering check against SIFTS, whose code is not published here, and what a re-run depends on. That allowance was made after the failure was seen. It was made because those three readings are decided by each computer's arithmetic (the sign of a height along an axis whose direction is arbitrary, and a sample at a window's edge), and the release's re-run still compares every class strictly, and every released radius to the hundredth.

    What version 0.1 can tell you about a variant

    In ChannelScope's README: What version 0.1 can tell you about a variant

    A new change at one of the 299 residues can be set beside the evidence already gathered there: the same structures, their states, and the variants already recorded. Of the expert-panel records at these residues, 222 are of uncertain significance; 213 of those are joined in at least one closed structure and 212 in at least one open one, on numbering that both checks agree on, and 9 in none; the 212 are all among the 213, so they can be read on structures of both classes.The 222 are at 200 residues. The one record ClinVar classifies as "VUS-high", which ClinVar presents as a sub-tier of uncertain significance whose criteria each submitter sets, is counted apart, never with these. These are counts of where the records can be read, and nothing is inferred from them.

    For a residue outside the set, ChannelScope's version 0.1 has no table and gathers no gnomAD or ClinVar evidence; joining its partners, the structures' numbering and their states, which are in the releases, has not been done or tested. And reading a new variant's effect from this evidence would first need the method checked against variants of known effect, benign ones among them, which version 0.1 has not done. For c.1840C>T, then, ChannelScope can say which residue 614 is in each species and in each structure joined to it, with the reason each other structure is not; the measured state of each of those structures; and the variants and classifications recorded at it, each value traceable to its source. What the change does to the channel rests on evidence of other kinds, such as laboratory tests. Nothing here changes a classification or applies any ACMG/AMP criterion.

    One published idea bears on the question. In the N-terminal region of RyR1 and RyR2, the largest cluster of disease changes faces the boundary between subunits, an interface that changes as the channel opens, and the Van Petegem lab proposed that weakening such an interface lowers the barrier to opening. It is their hypothesis, and this article does not test it.
